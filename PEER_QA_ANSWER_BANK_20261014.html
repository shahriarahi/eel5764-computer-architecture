Likely questions

Setup

Why use a simulator instead of real hardware?

gem5 gives us repeatable timing, a full view inside the cache, and the ability to change the hardware itself. That let us check that every cache block sat in its allowed ways, and later make the crossbar faster to find the leak. The trade-off is that gem5 is a timing model, not silicon. Real chips are much noisier, so the absolute sizes won't transfer, but the structural point (way masks don't divide the interconnect) should.

Evidence: paper §III and §X

Setup

Why such a small 4 KiB L1 data cache?

With a normal 16 KiB L1, the four 1 KiB AES tables stayed in the private cache, so almost no AES traffic reached the shared LLC and even our unpartitioned control could not see AES. Shrinking the L1 to 4 KiB pushes table lookups out to the LLC. It is a deliberate stress setup, and we list it as a limit on how far the result generalizes.

Evidence: paper §II and §X

Setup

Why T-table AES? Are you trying to break AES?

No. T-table AES just gives a controlled, well-known source of memory traffic. We use a public key and public inputs, and the monitor only decides whether AES is running or idle in each time interval. We never try to recover key bytes, and everything runs in simulation.

Evidence: paper §I and §II.B

Setup

What does "distinguishable" mean exactly?

Each run has 64 time intervals, half with AES active and half idle, in a fixed public schedule. We ask whether the monitor's probe latency differs between active and idle intervals more than chance allows, using 10,000 random relabelings as the null. We combine the three inputs per allocation and correct for testing 16 things at once, at a 5% error level.

Evidence: paper §V; slide 3

Setup

Why do you need matched controls?

Changing the allocation changes the monitor too, because a monitor with 8 ways walks 8 lines per set. So every partitioned run is compared with an unpartitioned run of the same monitor, inputs and schedule. The only difference in a pair is whether the way masks are on, and a partitioned result only counts if its control detects AES.

Evidence: paper §IV.B; slide 3

Setup

How do you know the partitioning was actually enforced?

We don't infer it from performance. A small gem5 extension tags every request with its core, and at each statistics dump we count which partition owns every valid LLC block and which way it sits in. All 24 partitioned runs had zero blocks outside their allowed ways, and in the mechanism study we checked this at all 64 interval snapshots of every run.

Evidence: paper §VI.B; slide 3

Setup

Why a simple in-order CPU instead of an out-of-order core?

A simple timing core issues one probe load at a time, so each probe walk's latency is easy to read and fully repeatable. An out-of-order core would overlap the loads and make single-walk timing harder to interpret. The leak we found lives in the shared cache path, not in the core, but repeating the study on an out-of-order core is reasonable future work.

Evidence: paper §III

The mistake

You first concluded 1/15 was secure. What went wrong?

Our first monitor saved its 16,384 measurements into a 384 KiB array while it was measuring. With only a few ways, those writes pushed out the monitor's own probe lines, so 20–25% of probe walks became slow misses, but only in partitioned runs. That noise hid a much smaller real signal. We withdrew the result, fixed the monitor, and re-ran everything under rules written down in advance.

Evidence: slide 4; paper §IV.C and §VII.B

The mistake

How do you know the new result isn't another artifact?

The fixed monitor stores each result inside the probe line it just loaded and keeps its loop state in registers, which we confirmed in the compiled code. Probe walks with a miss dropped to at most 0.02%, all in the very first interval. Beyond that, every analysis stage reproduces byte for byte, an independent reimplementation gives the same conclusions, and a fresh re-simulation matched bit for bit.

Evidence: slide 5; paper §VI; validation report

The mistake

Didn't you change the analysis after seeing the data?

Partly, and we say so in the paper. We added the hit-only statistic after looking at the October data, and we tightened the self-eviction check after the first corrected run. Both were written down before the final data existed. Also, the original statistic with our accepted proposal's exact rule gives the same answer at all eight allocations, so the conclusion doesn't depend on those additions.

Evidence: paper §V.C and §X

Results

0.3 cycles per line sounds tiny. Does it matter?

It is small per line, but it is consistent. At 8/8 a probe walk touches 8 lines, so it shifts by about 2.2 cycles, and over 64 intervals the difference is far beyond chance (corrected p at the test's floor). On real hardware, noise is larger, so an observer would need more samples. Our claim is that the channel exists, not that it is practical at this size on a real chip.

Evidence: slide 6; paper §VII

Results

Why does 1/15 keep 100% of the signal but larger allocations only 70–85%?

We measured that partial drop but have not fully explained it. The mechanism runs give one clue: at 8/8 with the normal crossbar, the control shows 0.40 and the partitioned run 0.28, but with a near-ideal crossbar both fall to the same 0.033. So the gap is tied to crossbar traffic. We did not isolate why partitioning trims part of it at larger allocations.

Evidence: paper §VII.A and §VIII

Results

Is there any allocation where partitioning does work?

Not between 1/15 and 8/8. Every allocation stays distinguishable, and none comes close to zero: our "no leak" margin was ±0.02 cycles per line, and every confidence interval sits around 0.2 to 0.35.

Evidence: slide 6; paper §VII

Results

What did partitioning cost?

Almost nothing here: at most 0.029% in monitor IPC and 0.008% in AES cycles per encryption, because both programs fit easily in their ways. A cache-hungry co-runner would likely show a real cost. The monitor's 0.26-point rise in LLC miss rate is a one-time refill at the start of measurement, not ongoing interference.

Evidence: slide 9; paper §IX

Results

Why only three seeds? Are they independent?

gem5 is deterministic, so the same input gives the identical result and repetition has to come from different inputs. Our three seeds change the AES plaintexts but share one activity schedule, so they are not fully independent, and we say so. The conclusion doesn't rest on combining them: each of the 24 partitioned runs is significant on its own, and still is after correcting for all 24.

Evidence: paper §X

Mechanism

So what actually leaks, if not evictions?

Timing on cache hits. At 1/15 with AES idle, 8,188 of 8,189 hit walks take exactly 33 cycles. With AES active, about 9% arrive 2, 4 or 6 cycles late, which is exactly one to three ticks of the shared crossbar's 1 GHz clock.

Evidence: slide 7; paper §VIII.A, Fig. 2b

Mechanism

What is the "crossbar request layer", and why is it shared?

Both cores' L1 caches reach the LLC through one crossbar, and that crossbar has a single request lane into the LLC. When an AES request holds the lane for 1 to 3 nanoseconds, a monitor request arriving at that moment waits. Way partitioning decides where lines are stored, not how requests reach the cache, so it never touches this lane.

Evidence: slide 8; paper §VIII

Mechanism

How did you show it's the crossbar and not something else?

We ran a preregistered test with four crossbar settings: default, wider, faster clock and near-ideal, 48 runs in total. The lane was busier in every AES-active interval, and the leak fell in exactly the same order as the lane's load (rank correlation 1.0). The near-ideal crossbar removed 100% of the leak at 1/15 and 88% at 8/8. Because the prediction of 100% at both failed, the preregistered outcome is "partial".

Evidence: slide 8; paper §VIII table and Fig. 2a

Mechanism

Is a "near-ideal crossbar" realistic?

No, and it isn't meant to be. It has a 512-byte datapath, no header delay and runs at the CPU clock. It is a diagnostic that shows where the leak lives, not a design we are proposing.

Evidence: paper §VIII

Mechanism

What is the small leftover at 8/8?

It is +0.033 cycles per line, identical with and without partitioning, and it still arrives in steps of one tick of the LLC's own 1 GHz clock. We attribute it, by elimination, to the LLC's own request path (its single port and response queue). That part is not directly measured, because gem5's cache model has no per-port queue statistic.

Evidence: paper §VIII.B

Mechanism

How would you actually close the leak?

Keep way partitioning, and also divide the interconnect into the LLC, for example by reserving bandwidth per security domain or giving each domain its own time slots on the shared lane. We did not test such a mitigation; it is the natural next step.

Evidence: slide 10; paper §VIII.B and §X.B

Scope

Does this mean real cache partitioning features are broken?

We can't speak for any specific product; our result is about this simulated system. What it shows is that way partitioning by itself separates storage, not the shared paths requests travel on. A defense needs both.

Evidence: paper §X

Scope

Can someone reproduce this?

Yes. Every run is scripted, the analysis rules and file hashes were written down before the final data, and each result reproduces byte for byte. A full sweep takes about 45 minutes of simulation; our runbook lists every command, and we're happy to share it.

Evidence: paper §X.A

Asking other groups good questions

  1. Baseline. "What is your baseline, and does it show the effect before your change?"
  2. Proof the mechanism is on. "How did you confirm your feature was actually active, beyond the performance change?"
  3. Noise. "How many runs did you use, and how did you decide a difference was real?"
  4. Sensitivity. "Would the conclusion hold at a different cache size, workload or core model?"
  5. Surprises. "What result surprised you most, and what would you test next?"
